# Profitability mechanisms: AI visibility and Shopify catalog apps

**Research snapshot: 2026-09-29.** The claim that ecommerce AI/SEO products are “not profitable” is not established by public evidence. The market is young, much of the relevant software is private, and public indicators such as funding, customer counts, list prices, ARR claims, review volume and team size do not disclose operating profit or unit gross margin. The useful question is which product and delivery choices can support attractive contribution economics, and which need to be measured before assuming they do.

## What vendor disclosures do and do not show

Peec AI announced **$10 million ARR**, 2,500-plus customers, more than 70 employees and $29 million total funding in May 2026. This is company-reported ARR (annualized recurring revenue), not audited recognized revenue, gross profit, cash flow or net income. It is nonetheless evidence that some buyers are paying for AI search analytics at a scale beyond a few hobby apps. The release does not disclose average contract value, gross margin, acquisition cost, retention, payroll expense or profitability. [Peec AI release](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/28/3303009/0/en/Peec-AI-hits-10M-ARR-16-months-after-launch-as-brands-race-to-optimize-for-AI-search.html)

Profound reported more than $155 million raised and a $1 billion valuation in February 2026, with 700-plus enterprise customers, then announced a further $180 million Series D at $1.8 billion in September 2026 and more than 1,000 enterprise brands. These primary company announcements establish financing and customer scale, not sales, recognized revenue, margins, or profit. They cannot support a conclusion that Profound is either profitable or losing money. [Profound Series C](https://www.tryprofound.com/newsroom/profound-raises-series-c-at-1b-valuation-to-lead-a-new-category-of-marketing) · [Profound Series D](https://www.tryprofound.com/newsroom/profound-raises-usd180m-series-d-at-usd1-8b-valuation-to-build-the-ai-platform-for-marketing-teams)

Scrunch’s official about page reports $26 million funding and 500-plus customers/agencies. Its company timeline says it grew from three to 49 employees during 2025; that is a useful signal about the possible people cost of enterprise coverage and go-to-market, not a P&L. Scrunch was acquired by Sitecore in 2026, but the public announcement does not disclose deal terms or Scrunch standalone earnings. No primary public source found for Peec, Profound or Scrunch provides audited net profit, gross margin, ARR cohort retention, support expense, or employee compensation expense. Do not backsolve those from valuation or headcount. [Scrunch company/about information](https://scrunch.com/about/)

Funding is cash raised in financing transactions, not revenue or profit. ARR is a run-rate measure and may differ from recognized revenue under accounting rules. Gross margin also cannot be inferred from a cheap model token: answer collection, retries, licensed data, hosting, customer success, human setup and research may dominate. Vendor scale says the category can attract substantial capital and paying customers; it does not prove a low-price Shopify app is a good business.

## Contribution-margin sensitivity in USD

The accompanying [profitability-scenarios.json](profitability-scenarios.json) is an editable calculation, not a forecast. Every amount is USD monthly. It applies Shopify’s published **2.9% billing processing charge** and assumes **0% revenue share** only for an eligible developer still within Shopify’s first $1 million of qualifying lifetime gross App Store revenue. Shopify says the first $1 million earns 0% share and the balance 15%; certain larger developers pay 15% from dollar one. It also calculates share on gross sales, excludes refunds, aggregates associated developer accounts, and may add regulatory fees. The $19 registration charge and tax/VAT are omitted from recurring per-customer calculations. [Shopify revenue-share rules](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/distribution/revenue-share)

For a 70% contribution-margin target, the amount left for all monthly support labor is:

```text
support_budget_USD = price × 0.30 − (price × 0.029) − delivery_cost − (monthly_churn × CAC)
support_minutes = support_budget_USD ÷ 30 USD/hour × 60
```

Here the 70% target is after card processing, direct delivery cost, expected replacement CAC, and support valued at **$30/hour**. A negative support allowance means only that the selected 70% contribution-margin target is missed before support; it does **not** by itself mean the customer is unprofitable or the business loses money. For example, the $9.99 monitor has $1.70 contribution per payer before support after the listed delivery and replacement CAC assumptions (about 17% of price), despite a negative $5.29 support allowance at the 70% target. Conversely, some managed-service rows already have negative contribution before support. “Delivery cost” is a scenario input for direct service/API delivery, not vendor-reported COGS. Replacement CAC is a simplifying steady-state monthly allowance: monthly logo churn × all-in acquisition cost per replacement. It excludes initial growth spend, fixed overhead, founder salary, taxes, failed payments, refunds, chargebacks, annual discounts and uncapped implementation. The CAC input should include the acquisition cost of any free audit cohort; never add it a second time.

| Illustrative model | Price | Delivery cost | Churn × CAC | Support allowance at target |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Self-serve app; 8% monthly churn, $20 CAC, $0.25 direct delivery | $9.99 | $0.25 | $1.60 | 1.7 min/month |
| Same self-serve assumptions | $19.99 | $0.25 | $1.60 | 7.1 min/month |
| Same self-serve assumptions | $49 | $0.25 | $1.60 | 22.9 min/month |
| Monitor; 5% monthly churn, $100 CAC, $3 delivery | $49 | $3 | $5 | 10.6 min/month |
| Same bounded monitor assumptions | $99 | $3 | $5 | 37.7 min/month |
| Managed repair; 3% monthly churn, $250 CAC, $15 delivery | $99 | $15 | $7.50 | 8.7 min/month |
| Same managed repair assumptions | $149 | $15 | $7.50 | 35.8 min/month |

The thresholds are intentionally demanding: the 70% target reserves remaining contribution for fixed costs, product work and profit. Under these assumptions, $9.99 self-service can tolerate under two support minutes per payer each month; a single ten-minute human touch costs $5 at the assumed loaded labor rate and breaks the target, though that alone does not prove a loss. $19.99 permits seven minutes. At $49, a mostly automated app has more room, but the same price supports only about eleven minutes of monthly support for the monitor case after $3 direct delivery and $5 replacement CAC. The $49 managed-repair case illustrates the distinction: before extra support, contribution is $49 − $1.421 processing − $15 delivery − $7.50 replacement CAC = **$25.079**, or about **51.18%** of price. It is positive but below the chosen 70% target, so its negative support allowance of $9.221 is target shortfall, not proof of an absolute loss. A $99 monitor affords 38 minutes, while $99 managed catalog work affords under nine minutes because its $15 delivery reserve and higher CAC consume most of the target envelope. If “managed repair” actually needs an hour of work ($30), the $99 case misses the target before extra support, though its contribution before that additional labor remains positive. A lower-priced product can still be profitable with self-serve delivery, low CAC from owned distribution, and controlled support; an adequately funded platform may also choose to spend heavily on growth while retaining positive customer-level contribution and recording an operating loss.

The scenarios are not market averages. The prior unit-cost note’s illustrative weekly collection cases—about $0.46 for 20 prompts across ChatGPT Search and Google SERP plus synthesis, and $1.14 for 50 prompts—show how an explicitly bounded monitor might sit within a $3 direct-delivery allowance. Those figures are product-specific examples at listed Apify rates, not a full measurement system or a quote for multiple answer engines. They omit retries, other collectors, crawl work, shared fixed infrastructure, support and any change in provider pricing. A 100-prompt daily single-collector stress workload reaches about $16.52 collection usage before synthesis in that note; it cannot safely be sold as if it cost the same as a weekly 20-prompt tier.

The key workload is **prompts × engines × geography × replicates × cadence**. A monitor with 50 prompts, three answer surfaces, two geographies, two replicate runs and four weekly measurements creates 2,400 prompt-engine-region-replicate observations per month. A price model that charges one “prompt” while silently including every axis gives the vendor unlimited exposure. Set explicit limits for each dimension, meter successful and failed attempts separately, cap retries, and bill overages or require an upgrade. Replication can reduce answer variance but multiplies collection use; geography may alter access or data-source requirements. A scheduled pull from one source is not a representative sample of every shopper’s experience.

Tavily Search is priced by search credits (listed docs describe one credit for basic/fast and two for advanced; pricing page should be checked at deployment). It finds web material and is useful for research or retrieval; it is not a measurement of what a particular ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity user saw. Apify’s named ChatGPT Search Scraper returns a specific collector’s search results/citations at its listed tier rates. Neither price can be generalized to all answer surfaces, consumer UI states, or autonomous browsing. A model-generated answer from a generic API is also not proof of the live answer-engine output. Keep collector cost distinct from synthesis tokens and from a complete measurement product. ## Product mechanics that can change the economics

**A low-priced Shopify app** can work if it removes work merchants do not want to repeat: install, import catalog, spot a fix, draft it safely, and show a clear result. Discovery in the App Store can lower cash CAC, but does not make CAC zero: listing work, support, free trials, audits, review management and onboarding all consume time. A free cohort should be measured as a funnel: total labor and infrastructure for all free stores divided by actual paid conversions. If 100 free audits cost $0.50 each and ten minutes of human help at $30/hour, the cohort costs $550. At 5% conversion, acquisition cost is $110 per paid customer before other marketing; at 1%, it is $550. Free users should have bounded scan depth, self-serve help and a visible paid next step. Conversion and eventual renewal, not downloads, decide whether this channel pays.

**A recurring monitor** can improve retention if merchants receive new, decision-relevant information and an action path each cycle. If the product only reveals a static visibility score or repeats noisy answers, customers may cancel after the initial curiosity period. Weekly sampling, prompt sets tied to product categories, source/citation deltas, and control groups can make repeat use meaningful. But measurement itself is not merchant value: prove that the recommended edits improve a merchant outcome or reduce labor. A benchmark cannot tell the founder’s product churn. ChartMogul’s December 2025 analysis of 3,500 software businesses reports median B2B SaaS NRR of 82% and 48–49% for AI-native and B2C products, with higher NRR among AI products above $250/month. NRR is annual revenue retained after contraction, churn **and expansion**, so it is not logo churn and does not translate directly into this model’s monthly churn input. It is a broad comparison only; actual price point, channel, stage and customer type matter. Do not plug these rates into a forecast. [ChartMogul NRR definition and benchmark](https://chartmogul.com/saas-metrics/nrr/)

**Managed catalog repair** trades software margin for larger contracts and customer outcomes. It can be more defensible when fixes require merchant approval, factual verification, theme/schema expertise, rollback, and clean before/after records. Yet recurring prices must fund recurring labor. Separate one-time catalog cleanup/onboarding fees from monitoring subscriptions; define product-count limits, turnaround time and revisions. Track minutes by task and rework, not only support inbox time. A service can be profitable with low gross margin if price and scope cover labor, but it is a different operating model from pure SaaS.

One structural option is **productized, evidence-led implementation**: sell a bounded initial catalog audit and approved fixes, then offer a capped monitor whose monthly report points to new changes or verifies a controlled sample. This can monetize work early while preserving a reason to renew. A stronger wedge than another generic score would be a fact-safe change queue: verified product facts, proposed edits, merchant approval, rollback, and a measured holdout or matched cohort. Tie results to incremental contribution profit, not just mentions, attributed orders, or gross channel revenue. Such a design may support $99–$149 pricing if it actually saves expert time or generates enough merchant margin; prices alone do not prove willingness to pay.

The first data to replace these assumptions should be: paid conversion by acquisition cohort, fully loaded CAC, monthly logo and revenue churn by plan, observed collection/retry cost per successful observation by source, median/p90 human minutes, onboarding hours, refund rate, and measured incremental merchant contribution. Until then, it is accurate to say that public vendor financials are opaque, some competitors show rapid customer/ARR growth, and attractive unit economics are possible only under bounded acquisition, support and measurement loads—not that the category is inherently unprofitable.
