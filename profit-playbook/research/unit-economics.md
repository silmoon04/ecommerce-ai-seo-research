# Unit economics: apparel, a functional alternative and eventual SaaS

Checked 30 September 2026. All three physical scenarios assume a UK seller, UK orders, adult items, domestic standard-card payments, one unit per order and a standard-delivery promise. The user’s location, legal status, VAT registration, supplier account prices, product choice and customer demand are unconfirmed. £72 crew, £45 tee and £30 tote are **delivered total prices to test**, not demand findings. The JSON alongside this note records the inputs, formulas and rounded results.

The schedule assigns tote bags 100g, making the listed UK shipping band £2.88. The £30 delivered total is a price hypothesis. It tests a useful carry item with a lower fulfillment cost, but the economics do not establish buyer interest or product quality. Shopify’s UK Basic plan lists standard online cards at 2% plus 25p; monthly billing is £25 (yearly billing shows £19/month). I apply only that payment rate to physical orders. The model treats the £72, £45 and £30 as each customer’s full receipt including delivery, so it does not add another shipping receipt. [Shopify UK pricing](https://www.shopify.com/uk/pricing).

UK distance sales require cancellation rights and a standard-delivery refund in relevant cases, so an accounting reserve does not replace the actual policy. ## Per-order model

| Delivered total | Gross profit before payment/refund/labor/marketing | Expected contribution before labor and acquisition | After valued order labor | CAC ceiling at break-even before fixed costs |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Changer crew, £72 | £36.69 | £28.53 | £26.03 | £26.03 |
| Freestyler tee + optional label, £45 | £20.92 | £15.69 | £13.19 | £13.19 |
| 300gsm tote, £30 | £15.99 | £12.46 | £10.46 | £10.46 |

“Gross profit” here means receipt less the direct product and outward-fulfilment cost only. Contribution further deducts the modeled Shopify fee and expected refund/replacement losses. At £15/hour, variable founder time is 10 minutes per clothing order and 8 minutes per tote order. The resulting CAC ceiling spends all unit contribution left after that labor and leaves nothing for monthly fees, fixed setup time, tax on profit or profit itself. A practical target must stay below the ceiling by enough to cover those amounts.

With the inherited illustrative cash-only proof budget of £297.24 and 108 fixed founder hours valued at £15/hour, economic break-even is 74 crews, 146 tees or 184 totes if all orders are organic. At £8 acquisition cost it becomes 107 crews, 370 tees or 780 totes. These are single-product cases, not a mixed sales plan. A denominator at or below zero means no finite break-even. To make the difference between contribution and a monthly result concrete, 10 orders of a single product at £8 CAC each, after variable labor and a £25 Shopify monthly fee, leave about £155 crew / £27 tee / negative £0 tote before setup labor, other overhead and taxes. This is a small illustrative operating result, not realized net profit. The fixed cash allowance comes from an earlier research scenario—samples £120, three months’ shop allowance £90, domain £15 and three delivered tee gifts £72.24. Replace it with the actual spending plan and do not count a refundable float or unused contingency as an expense.

## Price, refunds and marketing sensitivity

At unchanged delivery-inclusive price, a 10% price discount reduces pre-labor contribution to £22.05 crew, £11.64 tee and £9.76 tote. This is a steep margin reduction, especially for tees and totes. At 15% refund probability, contribution falls to £23.49 crew, £12.54 tee and £10.36 tote. The separate 2% replacement allowance remains in each row. Higher refunds, paid return labels, replacements with a second refund, or rejected supplier claims worsen the outcome. No price-response or refund distribution is known.

A proposed 10% creator commission is paid only after an order remains retained, using delivered receipt less the £3.95 delivery allowance. That is £6.81 per retained crew order, £4.11 per retained tee order and £2.60 per retained tote order. At the modeled 8% refund rate, the expected commission per initial acquired order is £6.26, £3.78 and £2.40 respectively; compare CAC and contribution on the same initial-order basis. Those are creator-acquisition costs, not extra product margin. Three gifted tees cost £72.24 at the modeled delivered tee cost; if five retained customers resulted, that is £14.45 per customer before commission. The customer count is only arithmetic. Add shipping, usage rights or a creator fee separately. No video reuse rate is verified; agreed usage should be priced as a fixed acquisition expense and attribution should be established before treating a sale as creator-generated.

“Free delivery” has no independent cost saving in this model because each stated price already includes delivery. If the item price were £72/£45/£30 **plus** delivery, receipts and card fees would change; if those totals remain fixed but delivery is advertised as free, fulfillment still comes out of the same price. An additional print location at £5.04 cuts contribution by about £5.04 before any recoverable VAT adjustment. Add decoration only if a higher price or measured increase in kept orders earns back the cost.

## VAT and cash availability

The base cash case is an unregistered seller: supplier VAT remains in cost and customer receipt is not split into output VAT. A registered UK seller has to separate output VAT on standard-rated goods and can reclaim only eligible input VAT with suitable records. For the same consumer price, the illustrative registered-case contribution before labor is £23.50 crew, £12.88 tee and £10.24 tote, versus £28.53, £15.69 and £12.46 unregistered. This simplification assumes all modeled receipts and costs are standard rated, inclusive of VAT, and fully recoverable where input VAT applies. The standard VAT rate is 20%; mandatory registration applies when taxable turnover exceeds £90,000 in the last 12 months or is expected to exceed that in the next 30 days, while voluntary registration below the threshold is possible. VAT place-of-supply and input-recovery rules can vary, so the model is not tax advice. [VAT rate](https://www.gov.uk/vat-rates), [registration](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat), [input VAT recovery](https://www.gov.uk/charge-reclaim-record-vat/reclaim-vat-business-expenses).

Cash receipts and margin arrive on different clocks. Inkthreadable publishes a 3–5 working-day production lead before dispatch. Shopify Payments lists a three-business-day minimum settlement for UK accounts, followed by typically 1–3 business days for the bank to process the transfer. Shopify says refunds reduce the next payout; a UK refund may also be debited from the linked bank account outside the normal payout schedule. Keep enough working cash to place supplier orders and refund customers through a slow payout or return window. There is no observed order history to justify a precise float. [Inkthreadable shipping](https://www.inkthreadable.co.uk/shipping-costs); [Shopify timing](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/payouts/payout-timing); [refund debits](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/payouts/refunds).

## SaaS workload, support and no LTV shortcut

The eventual SaaS example is a workload cost exercise, not an implementation or forecast. It assumes 100 active free accounts and 10 paid accounts at £49 per month including VAT. Every account gets 25 text reports of 12,000 input and 2,000 output tokens per month. At the official GPT-5.6 Luna published API prices ($0.20/M input and $1.20/M output), a chosen £0.75/$ conversion gives about 9p of model use per account for that workload. The actual model, prompt sizes, tools, retry rate, FX and deployment are unknown. [Official model pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-5.6-luna).

The key cost is support labor, not text-token spend. Five support minutes per free account costs £1.25 per month at the scenario’s £15/hour; across 100 free accounts, free-tier support plus model use is £134/month. Thirty minutes per paid account costs £7.50. After output VAT, Stripe’s standard UK card fee of 1.5% plus 20p, Stripe Billing’s pay-as-you-go 0.7% fee, support and direct model use, each paid account contributes about £31.97 before shared infrastructure, or 78.3% of £40.83 ex-VAT service revenue. At a fixed 100 free / 10 paid mix, six paid accounts would cover the £134 modeled free-tier support/API burden and £50 infrastructure allowance. If free accounts grow at a 10-to-1 ratio with paid accounts, three paid accounts would cover the corresponding free-tier burden plus £50 fixed infrastructure. Those answers differ because the first holds free accounts at 100 while paid accounts increase; the second holds the ratio fixed. For the illustrative 10-paid/100-free month, the partial operating surplus is about £135.65 after modeled API, payment, Billing, support and £50 infrastructure costs. It is not net profit: failed renewals, chargebacks, development, sales, administration, security, legal/accounting, usage overages and profit taxes remain excluded. The £50 allowance is a planning input, not a supplier quote. [Stripe UK payment fees](https://stripe.com/gb/pricing); [Stripe Billing fees](https://stripe.com/gb/billing/pricing).

A free tier should be self-serve or limited until real support time is known. It can cost more through people than inference. For paid plans, define report limits, support scope and overage rules; record workload and support minutes per paying account and free account. No retention cohort or customer lifetime has been measured, so there is no defensible LTV/CAC multiple. First establish paid conversion, renewal, support load and retained contribution over actual months.

## Small economic decisions

1. **Quote the exact variants before modeling as committed costs.** Pull a checkout quote for selected Changer/Freestyler colors, sizes, embroidery and delivery; revise the JSON cost input when it changes.
2. **Keep £72 delivered crew as a test, not a forecast.** It yields the highest modeled unit contribution, but a buyer’s willingness to pay is unknown. Change it only after a direct price test that records retained orders.
3. **Treat £45 delivered tee as a narrow-margin offer.** Its labor-adjusted acquisition ceiling is £13.19 before fixed costs; do not spend more to acquire a sale than the remaining margin allows.
4. **Test the label separately.** It adds £1.51 to cost. Keep only if buyers notice it or it improves kept-order quality enough to pay its way.
5. **Use the tote as one functional-product counterfactual.** A £30 delivered embroidered tote is easy to compare using the same storefront, but assess handling strength, print/embroidery and actual user interest before broader range work.
6. **Set discounts against contribution, not revenue.** At a 10% discount, the tee’s pre-labor margin falls to £11.64. Run discounts only with a measured target and a stop rule.
7. **Cap initial paid CAC below the modeled ceiling.** Reserve part of margin for fixed costs, taxes, founder time and a profit buffer. Stop if retained CAC reaches the full after-labor contribution.
8. **Treat affiliate commission as variable CAC.** Price creator codes on retained, non-refunded orders; compare total commission, gifted goods, postage and rights fee with order contribution.
9. **Do not promise a creator’s audience or content rights for free.** Obtain scope/fee and a trackable code or link; count sales only after reconciliation and refunds.
10. **Keep fulfillment and customer delivery inside one total.** Publish a clear delivered price and make sure the margin uses exactly the customer’s charged amount and carrier cost.
11. **Recalculate when refund outcomes mature.** Log cancellation, return, supplier reimbursement, reship and replacement-refund events separately; replace 8%/2% only with actual evidence.
12. **Value founder time in the go/no-go.** Show cash break-even and economic break-even side by side; the former alone hides the 108-hour fixed work assumption.
13. **Keep a ring-fenced cash reserve.** Plan for supplier payment before a payout, and refunds that may be debited separately. Reconcile actual settlement and supplier timing before increasing order volume.
14. **Monitor VAT threshold and price architecture.** Model output VAT before taxable turnover triggers registration, and test whether price can rise or margin can absorb VAT; do not assume supplier VAT is reclaimable while unregistered.
15. **Constrain SaaS free support and usage.** Measure AI workload and ticket minutes by account. Free access is not cheap if it requires human onboarding; price based on observed cost-to-serve.
16. **Use paid renewals before estimating SaaS lifetime value.** Wait for cohorts and churn evidence. A month’s contribution does not validate a lifetime multiple.
