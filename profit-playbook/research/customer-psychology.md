# Customer psychology: testable mechanisms, not conversion folklore

Checked 30 September 2026. This note applies original consumer and information-systems research to a proposed UK premium, understated anime/dark-fantasy clothing label and to a possible ecommerce AEO SaaS. Other physical goods and AI-assisted shopping are included where the same mechanism may travel. These studies give hypotheses, not a sales forecast. No cited study directly establishes demand for this brand or for a new AEO tool.

## What the research can and cannot tell us

Clothing can communicate identity, but the wearer’s meaning and an observer’s interpretation are not guaranteed to match. Buyers also infer quality from multiple cues; a premium price by itself is not proof of quality. Online fit and product uncertainty can be reduced with relevant detail, but assurances and generous-sounding return language do not always help. More options do not universally overwhelm people. The practical task is to learn which uncertainty blocks this audience, for this offer, at the real price.

### 1. Clothing can express identity, but the buyer controls the meaning

Feinberg, Mataro and Burroughs’ two studies found that observers could infer some social information from personally selected outfits, with those inferences correlating with wearers’ self-views. But social inferences from jeans labels did not map cleanly onto the identity of people who would wear those jeans. This is a small, older study, not a purchase test. A survey of 1,596 French adolescents linked personal and social identity to clothing importance, but its population and self-reported design limit generalization. [Feinberg et al. (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X9201100103); [Badaoui et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1002/cjas.1397).

**Test:** Interview fashion-first and fandom-first people separately. Show the actual garment and ask them to describe what it says about the wearer, then explain the reference and ask what changed. Compare an understated motif with a more overt version at equal size, styling and price. Track spontaneous interpretation and real choice, not agreement with the founder’s intended meaning. For physical products, ask what the object says about its owner; for AI-assisted discovery, ask what product detail makes the item personally relevant.

**Ask:** “Where would you wear this?” “What, if anything, does it say about the person wearing it?” “What would make this feel like your kind of thing?” “What would you wear or buy instead?”

### 2. A quiet signal only works when the intended group can read it

Han, Nunes and Drèze’s four studies concern established luxury brands and status signaling. Their model distinguishes buyers by both wealth and status motive: some segments value subtle marks recognizable to insiders, while others prefer conspicuous marks. This is evidence for a possible segment difference, not a general law that subtle design is desirable. A set of seven experiments on luxury labels found social treatment effects in some contexts, but a later extended replication did not reproduce positive label effects in its field experiment. [Han et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.74.4.15); [Nelissen & Meijers (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.12.002); [Bliege Bird et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0170216).

**Test:** Compare artwork variants that differ in reference legibility, not overall craft or photography. Ask participants to pick one they would wear outside a convention, then whether a knowledgeable fan would recognize it. Do not infer insider recognition from likes. For a SaaS landing page, test whether a merchant understands a specific example/output without generic “AI visibility” language.

**Ask:** “Would you want a stranger, another fan, or nobody to recognize the reference?” “Which part gives it away?” “Does the more obvious version change whether you would wear it day to day?”

### 3. “Self-expression” copy is not itself a demand signal

In a survey model of 338 US university students evaluating customized apparel, need for uniqueness and self-monitoring related to customization perceptions and extended-self; explicit self-expression and other-directedness were not significant predictors in that sample. Perceived customization and extended-self related to purchase intention. It was a convenience sample using hypothetical intentions, and customization is not the same as original fandom artwork. [Seo & Lang (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1108/JFMM-10-2018-0128).

**Test:** Offer one small, specific choice (e.g. thread colour or garment colour) against a fixed design, only if operationally feasible. See whether buyers select it and accept the price/time implications. For SaaS, test whether users value control over their own product facts, URLs and claim approvals, rather than merely responding to “tailored” positioning.

**Ask:** “Have you changed or customized a garment before? What did you change, and why?” “Would you want this choice, or would you rather have us decide?”

### 4. Premium quality needs observable evidence

Zeithaml’s synthesis describes price, perceived quality and perceived value as related but distinct. Small apparel experiments found participants use cues such as style, price, fiber labels, seller and designer names when judging clothing quality; they do not establish that higher prices reliably cause purchases. A 40-person controlled sweater study and simulated blouse tasks have limited external validity. Treat price as one cue among several, not as evidence that the garment merits it. [Zeithaml (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298805200302); [Heisey (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X9000800404); [Davis (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X8700600102).

**Test:** At the intended £72 crew price, show either a clean hero image alone or the same image plus verified fabric weight/composition, garment measurements, embroidery close-up and wash-tested sample detail. Keep all other content equal. Compare add-to-basket or, before launch, qualified choice and willingness to place a refundable, honestly described reservation; also record what people think is missing. For other physical goods, replace apparel facts with category-specific proof. For SaaS, show a real source-linked answer, change log and verified/unknown distinction.

**Ask:** “What makes you think this is well made?” “What would you need to see to judge the fabric, stitching or fit?” For SaaS: “What would convince you this report is accurate enough to act on?”

### 5. Price is both a cue and a cost to the buyer

Price-quality research does not support “raise the price and perceived quality rises” as a dependable tactic. Zeithaml’s means-end model treats value as what a customer receives relative to what they give up. In apparel experiments, price influenced predicted quality alongside design, fiber and seller cues, but results came from small, simulated shopping tasks. No cited work tells us whether £72 is viable here. [Zeithaml (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298805200302); [Heisey (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X9000800404).

**Test:** Show the same sample, evidence and delivery terms at a small set of preselected real prices, randomized between respondents. Ask for a choice against “buy nothing” and an alternative they actually use. Where feasible, advance with a real, clearly disclosed purchase or refundable reservation; stated willingness-to-pay is weaker evidence. Track expected contribution after fulfillment and returns, not preference alone. SaaS tests should compare a concrete free baseline with one paid tier tied to a defined task, not a price-only survey.

**Ask:** “What would you compare this price with?” “At what price would you doubt the quality? At what price would it be too much for you?” “What would have to be included for that price to make sense?”

### 6. Diagnostic product information can reduce uncertainty

In an eBay Motors study, Dimoka, Hong and Pavlou matched transaction data with primary survey responses from 331 bidders. Product uncertainty was distinct from seller uncertainty and had a stronger relationship with price premiums; diagnostic descriptions and third-party assurances helped reduce uncertainty. Used cars are a high-cost, different-category context, so the direction is informative but the effect cannot be transplanted to clothing. A field study of apparel review changes found that fit opinions work differently depending on the reviewer’s fit reference, supporting contextual fit information over a bare average rating. [Dimoka et al. (2012)](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol36/iss2/6/); [Wang et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1287/ISRE.2020.0965).

**Test:** On the sample product page, give actual garment measurements, model height/size, fit description, composition, close-ups and delivery timing. Compare to a thinner page only after enough relevant visitors exist; before that, use task-based interviews to learn what remains unclear. For SaaS, show which source supports each answer and flag stale, missing or conflicting product facts. For AI-shopping claims, record whether systems reproduce only facts in your dataset, and whether customers understand each surfaced attribute.

**Ask:** “What could you not tell from these photos?” “How would you decide which size to order?” “Which fact would change your decision?” “What answer would you want an AI shopping assistant to give, and what source would you trust?”

### 7. Choice reassurance can outperform pressure, but “no risk” language can backfire

A randomized field experiment in online retail compared self-assurance nudges that helped shoppers validate fit/style choices with pressure tactics such as scarcity and social persuasion. Self-assurance improved short-term sales and reduced returns in that retailer; scarcity pressure produced slightly higher short-term sales but more returns. Its intervention and customer base are specific, so treat it as a reason to test return-adjusted outcomes. Separate field experiments with over 452,000 consumers found that “take no risk” style return-policy messages reduced engagement and purchases in retargeting ads, apparently by making persuasive intent salient. That was an ad context, not a product-page return policy. [Ghose et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437231180494); [Woitmann & Steils (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2024.104134).

**Test:** Prefer plain fit guidance and clear, accurate returns information to countdowns, “everyone is buying this” claims or repeated “risk-free” copy. If traffic permits, compare a fit-check module with the existing page and use retained contribution after refunds/returns as the outcome. For SaaS, make trial limits, data access and cancellation terms visible; test task completion and trust, not click-through alone.

**Ask:** “What still feels risky about ordering?” “Would any of these details help you choose a size, or do they feel like sales language?” For SaaS: “What would you want to try before paying?” “What would make you cautious about connecting your store?”

### 8. More options sometimes help and sometimes hurt

Iyengar and Lepper’s 2000 lab and field experiments found greater motivation with limited sets in their jam, chocolate and essay contexts. But Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd’s meta-analysis of 50 experiments (5,036 participants) found the average effect of assortment size was nearly zero with substantial variation. Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman’s later meta-analysis found overload more likely with complex sets, harder decisions, uncertain preferences and effort-minimizing goals. These findings rule out a universal “fewer is always better” prescription. [Iyengar & Lepper (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995); [Scheibehenne et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235); [Chernev et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002).

**Test:** Start with two or three distinct designs as planned, but preserve “none” and expose the broader catalogue when it exists. In a prototype, compare a curated first view with the same options grouped by motif/garment; measure choice time, deferral, confidence and product selection. In SaaS, compare a short recommended queue with a sortable full audit list; monitor whether merchants resolve issues or stop. This tests navigation and decision burden, not the hypothesis that fewer is inherently better.

**Ask:** “How would you choose between these?” “What feels similar or different?” “Would you want more options, or is something missing from this set?”

### 9. Authenticity may be a baseline expectation rather than a bonus

Three experiments with 1,617 participants, including a preregistered study and a nationally representative sample, compared authentic, inauthentic and neutral-brand information. Authentic brands scored better than inauthentic ones, but did not outperform the neutral baseline. The finding suggests avoiding obvious inconsistency may matter more than claiming “authenticity” as a special selling point. The tested manipulations and stated responses do not establish repeat purchase. [Kokkoris & Kamleitner (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2025.104469).

**Test:** Show a specific, verifiable design/process note beside a vague “made by fans, for fans” claim, with all else equal. Ask whether either helps assess the offer; do not invent creator histories, fandom credentials, artist claims or licensed status. For SaaS, show exactly which records feed an answer, what is inferred, and what remains unknown; assess whether users can detect errors and correct them.

**Ask:** “What do you mean when you call a fandom product authentic?” “Which details would make you trust or question that?” SaaS: “What would make this explanation feel credible rather than promotional?”

### 10. People can infer persuasion motives from the message

Campbell and Kirmani’s experiments found that accessible persuasion motives and consumers’ available attention influenced how they judged a salesperson. Kirmani and Zhu found that suspicion cues can make some consumers more vigilant to manipulative intent. This does not mean every sales message or disclosure causes backlash; it means urgency and vague superlatives can invite motive inference, especially when shoppers scrutinize the claim. The experiments concerned sales/advertising contexts, not this brand. [Campbell & Kirmani (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1086/314309); [Kirmani & Zhu (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.44.4.688).

**Test:** Compare a factual product explanation with an emotionally framed version while holding imagery and offer constant. Ask what seems credible, what sounds exaggerated, and what evidence a participant would expect. For a SaaS page, disclose limitations and show an example where the tool says “unknown” instead of using unsupported claims about AI rankings or traffic. Measure qualified interest and comprehension; never optimize fake urgency or unverifiable promises.

**Ask:** “What part of this sounds like a claim that needs proof?” “What would you want us to show to back it up?” “Is any wording trying too hard to persuade you?”

### 11. Explanations and user control can support trust in shopping software

Wang and Benbasat empirically tested how explanation features in ecommerce recommendation agents affect initial trust beliefs. Explanation types covered how an agent works, why a recommendation was made, and trade-offs. Related algorithm-aversion experiments found that allowing people to make even small adjustments to an imperfect algorithm’s output can increase willingness to use it. Neither establishes SaaS willingness to pay, or trust in modern generative AI; it motivates transparent, editable recommendations rather than black-box scores. [Wang & Benbasat (2007)](https://doi.org/10.2753/MIS0742-1222230410); [Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643).

**Test:** Prototype an AEO finding with (a) the source URL/snippet, (b) the reason it matters to the merchant’s chosen task, and (c) a one-click way to correct, dismiss or defer. Compare that with a score-only screen in moderated tasks. Record successful verification, corrections, time and repeat use. For AI-assisted product discovery, expose why a product matched the stated need and let the shopper edit constraints. Do not use “AI recommended” as a quality claim.

**Ask:** “How would you check whether this answer is right?” “What would you change before publishing?” “Would you trust this more with an explanation, or does the explanation add work?”

## Suggested learning order

Begin with interviews and a physical sample: learn what identity means to each audience, whether the reference is understood, and which quality or fit facts are missing. Then test actual prices and a real purchase/reservation only when fulfillment is credible. For the SaaS, run merchant task tests on real store data with source links and edit control before testing landing-page language or pricing. In every test, preserve a “none” or “not useful” answer and report counts alongside percentages. Stated preference, prototype task completion, paid orders, retained contribution, and repeat use are different evidence levels; do not pool them into one demand claim.
