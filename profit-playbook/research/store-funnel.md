# Store funnel: small apparel and ecommerce UX

Research date: 30 September 2026. This note translates primary Baymard, Google web.dev/Search Central, W3C, and Shopify guidance into low-cost decisions for a new, low-traffic apparel brand. It is a queue of hypotheses and checks, not a claim that any single UX change guarantees a conversion lift.

## What the evidence can and cannot tell us

Baymard’s apparel work draws on moderated usability testing and benchmark audits of established retailers; its 2026 apparel quantitative article reports a survey of 1,922 US online shoppers. These are useful for spotting recurring uncertainty and interface failures, but neither the observed benchmark percentages nor survey answers predict this brand’s conversion rate or prove a causal uplift. The new store has no customers, no first-party fit feedback, and no validated willingness to pay, so product-specific claims need to be earned from a real sample and customer evidence. [Baymard apparel research and method](https://baymard.com/research/apparel-and-accessories), [2026 apparel quantitative insights](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-and-accessories-quantitative-ux-insights-2026)

Use small task-based sessions and customer questions to discover confusion, then use live funnel counts to see whether the issue occurs in practice. At low traffic, treat any one-week or one-order change as a signal to investigate, not a statistically reliable A/B result. Keep contribution after production, fulfilment, payment fees, discounts, returns and acquisition cost beside conversion and average order value; growth in either can still lose money.

## Decisions and inexpensive checks

1. **Build a truthful proof stack before adding persuasion widgets.** Put the actual garment, fabric, embroidery detail, construction, packaging and maker/sample evidence in view. Make quality statements only to the degree a sample inspection supports them. For a new direct-to-consumer brand, on-site reviews may carry less weight than independent confirmation, and Baymard observed users continuing to explore DTC sites even where reviews were absent. Do not fill an empty review area with invented, seeded, or family reviews. Ask early buyers for honest feedback after delivery and make its source clear. **Check:** ask five target shoppers what they trust and what they still need to verify after a short product-page visit; record questions, not purchase intent as a forecast. [Baymard on DTC reviews](https://baymard.com/research-articles/user-reviews-dtc)

2. **Show the garment on a person and in useful detail.** Pair a clean product view with a model/lifestyle view, close-ups of embroidery and seams, and views that show the shape and drape. Use the actual production sample; illustrative or generated imagery must not imply a different garment or finish. Baymard’s apparel tests identify human-model imagery as helpful because a flat cut-out alone makes fit and appearance harder to judge. **Check:** ask a shopper to describe the item’s fit, material, decoration and likely use from the page; note mismatches against the physical sample. [Baymard apparel best practices](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-5-best-practices)

3. **Put size guidance beside the size choice, using real garment measurements.** Publish a legible garment chart in centimetres, state whether numbers describe the garment or the wearer, include a simple measuring instruction, and explain the intended cut (for example, relaxed or oversized) only if verified. Add the model’s height and worn size when known. Keep the guide available without losing the selected variant or forcing an information hunt. **Check:** give shoppers a measurement scenario and ask them to choose a size; log the point where they hesitate and compare their answer with the sample’s measured dimensions. Baymard found sizing information is commonly incomplete and recommends a size-guide link near the selector. [Baymard apparel best practices](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-5-best-practices)

4. **Make available sizes visible as separate, accessible controls.** Prefer clearly labelled buttons over a collapsed size dropdown; show unavailable sizes as unavailable and avoid letting a shopper choose an invalid combination. This makes stock and choice easier to scan, especially on a phone. Baymard observed users overlooking dropdown size controls and being surprised by availability. **Check:** in a phone-sized task session, ask shoppers to find their size and switch colour without assistance; measure errors and time-to-correct-choice. [Baymard apparel best practices](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-5-best-practices)

5. **Answer delivery-cost and timing questions before cart.** Near the buy area, state the actual UK delivery charge, dispatch/estimated delivery window, and any free-delivery condition. If the timing is uncertain because production is made to order, describe the production and dispatch window plainly. Do not advertise “free” delivery when its cost is simply hidden in price; compare the landed contribution under both presentations before changing price. Baymard reports that shoppers seek shipping price and expected delivery information early; its checkout research cites extra charges as a common abandonment reason, but its survey is not a forecast for this store. **Check:** ask a shopper to estimate their final delivered cost and arrival window from the PDP; target zero material surprises at checkout. [Baymard product-page UX](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux), [Baymard checkout UX](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-of-checkout-ux)

6. **Make returns easy to find and economically explicit.** Link the actual return policy near the purchase decision and in the footer; state the return window, eligible condition, how to start a return, who pays return postage, and refund/exchange handling. Have the policy checked for the seller’s jurisdiction and product model before launch; do not promise a generous policy that the unit economics cannot support. Baymard research says users look for this information on PDPs and in footers, with policy concerns affecting consideration. **Check:** ask shoppers to find the return rule without prompting; separately model expected contribution at plausible return-rate scenarios. [Baymard product-page UX](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux), [Baymard shipping and returns links](https://baymard.com/research-articles/footer-needs-return-shipping-links)

7. **Separate verified product facts from brand story.** Put scannable material composition, care instructions, garment weight or thickness only if supplier/sample data confirms it, print/embroidery method, and fit notes close to the decision. Keep narrative copy concise and label what is a design intention versus a tested property. The test is whether a shopper can answer “what will arrive?” without translating mood copy into product facts. **Check:** after a five-second glance, ask a shopper what they expect to receive and what remains unknown; correct mismatches before buying more photography or copy.

8. **Keep the decision essentials visible; collapse only supporting detail.** Product name, price, colour and size choice, stock status, delivery cue, returns link, and add-to-cart action should be easy to locate. Longer material, care, production, and policy details can use consistent titled accordions, provided they remain keyboard operable and searchable. Baymard supports collapsed sections for long PDP content as an overview aid; that is not a reason to hide material purchase constraints. **Check:** ask shoppers to locate each critical fact in turn, on mobile and desktop, and remove repeated or low-value modules that delay those tasks. [Baymard ecommerce UX best practices](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-ux-best-practices)

9. **Treat mobile product photography as both evidence and a performance budget.** Serve image sizes appropriate to the viewport, reserve image dimensions to prevent layout shifts, and prioritize the first useful product image. Google web.dev notes desktop-sized images can use 2–4 times more data than needed on mobile; Google’s “good” Core Web Vitals thresholds are LCP ≤2.5s, INP <200ms and CLS ≤0.1 at the 75th percentile. These are user-experience targets, not a guarantee of rankings or sales. Use field data as it becomes available; lab checks are directional and cannot reproduce all real-user conditions. **Check:** test a representative phone/network profile and inspect field metrics once enough eligible visits accumulate. [web.dev responsive images](https://web.dev/articles/serve-responsive-images), [Google Search Central Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals), [web.dev field and lab measurement](https://web.dev/articles/vitals-measurement-getting-started)

10. **Use an accessible mobile control baseline.** Make size/colour selectors, quantity, gallery controls, cart and checkout usable by keyboard and touch, preserve a visible focus state, label form fields and errors, and ensure controls meet WCAG 2.2 AA target-size rules (24 by 24 CSS pixels or compliant spacing). For primary touch actions, use larger targets where the layout permits. **Check:** complete a PDP-to-checkout task using only keyboard, then on a phone; note inaccessible choices, unclear errors, missed focus and accidental taps. WCAG provides a minimum compliance criterion, not a complete usability guarantee. [W3C WCAG 2.2 target size](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum), [W3C WCAG 2.2 guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/)

11. **Keep checkout simple and show total cost early.** Use a prominent guest path, request only needed information, preserve cart contents, expose delivery choice and full total before payment, and make errors specific and recoverable. Prefer platform-native checkout for an initial one-product store over custom checkout work unless a measured blocker justifies the cost. Baymard’s large-site benchmark identifies guest checkout, cost transparency, and fewer form elements as recurring patterns. Their observed average fields are not a target for a tiny store, and local payments/shipping needs must be checked in the actual UK checkout. **Check:** run a first-time mobile checkout task with a shopper who has no account; record required fields, surprises, errors, and whether they can recover. [Baymard checkout UX best practices](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-of-checkout-ux), [Shopify checkout guide](https://www.shopify.com/blog/ecommerce-checkout)

12. **Do not bundle just to raise AOV.** First establish whether a genuinely complementary item (for example, a tee and crew with distinct use cases) increases contribution per visitor after discount, pick/pack, shipping, return and inventory costs. In a small catalogue, a simple “complete the set” offer can be manually shown before paying for bundle software. Shopify describes bundles as a way to lift AOV and provide curation, while its documentation also calls out variant, inventory and return considerations. Treat AOV as a diagnostic, not the objective. **Check:** compare manual offer take-up and contribution per session/order against standalone sales; stop if extra units or a discount reduce contribution or create confusing size choices. [Shopify product bundles](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/bundles), [Shopify bundle eligibility and considerations](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/bundles/eligibility-and-considerations)

13. **Make post-purchase reassurance the first follow-up.** Send a clear confirmation, dispatch/tracking updates, a useful care note, and an easy support/return path. Invite an honest review only after delivery and enough use to judge the item; disclose any incentive if used and do not require positive feedback. Add a relevant cross-sell only once it is genuinely useful and does not obscure order status or support. Shopify describes confirmation, tracking, returns, product education, and later review requests as post-purchase touchpoints; its reported case studies are vendor examples, not transferable lift estimates. **Check:** review support contacts, delivery complaints, return reasons, and opt-outs before adding more automated messages. [Shopify post-purchase experience](https://www.shopify.com/blog/post-purchase-experience-ecommerce)

14. **For a low-traffic store, use task evidence before split tests.** Start with five to eight short, moderated sessions among plausible buyers; rotate which task comes first and capture hesitation, failed choices, and questions. Fix clear usability failures, then instrument product view → variant selection → add-to-cart → checkout start → purchase, plus refund/return reason and contribution. Report counts and denominators. A handful of sessions can find usability issues, but cannot establish market demand; a handful of orders cannot establish causal lift. Use an A/B test only once traffic and conversion volume make a predeclared minimum detectable effect practical. **Check:** every proposed change names one expected behavior, one guardrail (such as contribution or returns), a measurement window, and a stop condition before release.

15. **Transfer the onboarding lesson to AEO SaaS only where the user’s task matches.** A future merchant-facing SaaS can borrow the store’s information hierarchy: show one concrete example of the output, ask for only the minimum facts needed to produce it, explain unknowns and verification status, and expose a recoverable next step. This is an analyst inference from ecommerce uncertainty-reduction and form-friction findings, not an evidenced SaaS conversion claim. Do not port apparel-specific size or imagery patterns into SaaS. **Check:** observe whether a target merchant can reach and understand one useful output without setup help; track time-to-first-value and completion alongside activation quality, not just sign-ups. [Baymard checkout/form usability](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-of-checkout-ux)

## Source limits and interpretation

- Baymard’s retailer benchmark percentages describe audited large/established sites and dated study samples. They identify common failure modes, not expected lifts for a new store. Qualitative observation establishes that a problem can occur; it does not establish its frequency in this audience.
- The 2026 Baymard apparel survey is US shopper self-report. The store’s intended first market is modelled as UK only; verify shipping, return wording, payment methods and customer expectations against actual UK operations and applicable rules before launch.
- Shopify articles are useful first-party platform guidance and sometimes include merchant case studies or third-party studies. A vendor-reported outcome does not establish the same result for another merchant. Shopify help documents describe the platform’s current feature constraints and can change.
- Google’s Core Web Vitals are field-user experience metrics and thresholds; Google does not promise that meeting them causes a ranking or conversion improvement. Lab and field results can differ, especially at low traffic.
- W3C WCAG criteria are normative accessibility criteria. Passing the cited target-size criterion does not make an entire purchase flow accessible; test the complete task with assistive technology and real users when possible.
- The proposed session counts, event funnel, decision gates, and stop rules above are analyst-designed operating choices, not findings from these sources. Keep thresholds provisional until the store has baseline data.
