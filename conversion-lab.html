Choose a merchant's baseline, the contribution from a completed order and a hypothetical effect. Then ask whether the value could support a fee, and whether the effect is measurable.

Every input below is an assumption, not a market average or a forecast. A price supported by this arithmetic is a price to test with a buyer; it is not evidence of willingness to pay.

How these numbers are calculated

Baseline orders = eligible monthly visits × baseline purchase conversion.

Extra orders = baseline orders × assumed relative change. Extra contribution = extra orders × contribution per completed order.

Break-even relative lift = proposed fee ÷ baseline order contribution. The fee ceiling divides extra contribution by the chosen value / fee multiple. If extra contribution is zero or negative, it supports no positive fee.

Merchant benefit = extra contribution minus the proposed fee. These outputs assume the intervention caused the entire conversion change and the contribution per order stayed the same. They omit fixed implementation costs unless you account for those separately.

Could you measure that effect?

80% power · 5% two-sided significance

This normal approximation uses the baseline and relative change above, equal allocation and independent purchase outcomes. It is a planning floor.

Repeat visits and product or merchant clusters reduce independence. Testing 50 hypotheses introduces multiplicity. Model reruns are not independent human shoppers. None of those adjustments, ramp-up time or the delay needed to observe returns is included here.

Sample-size formula and limits

For baseline probability p₁ and assumed probability p₂, let p̄ = (p₁ + p₂) / 2.

n per arm = ceil((1.95996398454 × √(2p̄(1 − p̄)) + 0.84162123357 × √(p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂)))² / (p₂ − p₁)²)

Minimum months = 2n ÷ eligible monthly visits. This assumes the eligible visits represent independent units and are allocated equally. Zero change has no finite sample size in this model. Rates at 0% or 100% need a different planning method.
