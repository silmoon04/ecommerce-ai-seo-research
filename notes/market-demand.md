# Market demand: AI discovery for ecommerce merchants

**Research date:** 28 September 2026  
**Question:** Is there real, monetizable demand for a tool that helps ecommerce merchants get found or recommended in AI shopping, and could it be profitable?  
**Scope:** Public primary or first-party empirical sources. Growth rates are not treated as market size. This note distinguishes AI-referred visits, AI-influenced purchases, AI-search visibility, and merchants’ willingness to pay.

## Finding

There is a real and growing change in shopping discovery, with evidence that AI recommendations can influence later site visits and that AI-referred sessions can be unusually valuable. That establishes a merchant problem worth testing. It does **not** establish that independent online shops currently lose enough sales, or that they will pay a standalone subscription to fix it. The best current estimate puts generative-AI referrals at just 0.4% of retail ecommerce visits; rising multiples mostly reflect a small starting denominator. Consumer influence is broader than click-through, but it is difficult to attribute and even harder to promise as incremental revenue. Shopify is also moving core product-catalog distribution and AI-channel reporting into its platform, reducing the defensibility of a thin “AI readiness” or listing app.

My read: viable as a narrowly targeted, low-cost SaaS or service-led product if it produces measurable actions and incremental merchant revenue; not yet supported as a broad high-ARPU market for generic GEO monitoring. A credible business case has to be demonstrated store-by-store, then converted to paid renewals. No available public survey proves demand or willingness to pay specifically for ecommerce GEO software.

## What the evidence says

### AI-referral traffic: fast growth from a small base

Adobe Analytics reports that generative-AI links sent traffic to U.S. retail sites up **693.4% year over year during Nov. 1–Dec. 31, 2025**. Adobe says its analysis covers more than **1 trillion visits to U.S. retail sites**, 100 million SKUs, and 18 product categories; it explicitly cautions that the AI-traffic base remained “modest.” This is click-through traffic to Adobe-covered retail sites, not all AI-influenced shopping, nor a count of independent merchants. [Adobe holiday report, 7 Jan 2026](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/01/adobe-holiday-shopping-season)

Adobe’s June 2026 update says U.S. retail traffic from AI sources was up **1,324% since October 2024** and **138% year over year in May 2026**. That update repeats the same broad coverage claim (>1 trillion retail visits) and adds a March 2026 survey of 5,000+ U.S. consumers. Its 1,324% cumulative increase is meaningful directionally, but not an estimate of the addressable revenue pool. [Adobe Digital Insights, 17 Jun 2026](https://business.adobe.com/uk/blog/adobe-report-ai-traffic-travel-sites-surges-200-percent)

Independent commercial measurement from Similarweb offers a needed denominator: its 2026 State of Ecommerce report says generative AI referrals grew **203% year over year** but supplied only **0.4% of retail ecommerce visits**. The public report page does not show the underlying time window, store panel, country mix, or detailed method, and the report is co-written with Statista. Treat 0.4% as a useful scale check, not a precise universal benchmark. [Similarweb, State of Ecommerce 2026](https://www.similarweb.com/corp/reports/state-of-ecommerce-2026/)

Similarweb’s separate global top-1,000-sites analysis counted 1.13 billion AI referrals in June 2025 versus 191 billion Google-search referrals; the AI figure was up 357% YoY. It is explicitly a top-1,000 website sample, global, and dominated by large sites. The comparison reinforces both momentum and the small current click pool; it cannot stand in for small-shop traffic. [Similarweb, 29 Jul 2025](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/ai-referral-traffic-winners/)

### Clicked referrals can be high quality—but benchmarks vary

Adobe published different comparisons across its reports, so these figures should not be stitched into a single trend. Its March 2025 report said February 2025 AI referrals converted 9% below non-AI traffic (the same report compared that with a 43% gap in July 2024). The June 2025 Q2 report reported a 22% deficit in May 2025, but described July 2024 as a 91% deficit. That baseline differs materially from the earlier report; the public material does not explain the discrepancy. The June report also put May revenue per visit at 70% of non-AI. Prefer the more recent, separately dated results below over a smoothed growth narrative. [Adobe, 17 Mar 2025](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2025/03/17/adobe-analytics-traffic-to-us-retail-websites-from-generative-ai-sources-jumps-1200-percent); [Adobe Q2 2025 analysis, 24 Jun 2025](https://business.adobe.com/uk/blog/ai-driven-traffic-surges-ahead-in-q2)

By May 2026 Adobe reported AI referrals converting **54% better** than non-AI sources and producing **53% higher revenue per visit**. In March it reported +42% conversion and +37% revenue per visit. Adobe’s data covers U.S. retail behavior across a very large retail-site dataset; conversion comparisons are observational and aggregate. They do not prove that optimization caused the lift, and channel mix/category/customer intent may explain part of it. [Adobe update, 17 Jun 2026](https://business.adobe.com/uk/blog/adobe-report-ai-traffic-travel-sites-surges-200-percent)

Similarweb’s 2025 consumer-transactional-site data also found roughly 7% conversion for AI referrals versus 5% for Google referrals, but that is a broader transactional-site measure, not a direct Shopify small-store result. Its own later State of Ecommerce page emphasizes a different, more relevant combination metric: journeys involving both AI and search converted at 23%, 84% above journeys using AI alone. That suggests AI often assists comparison and validation while conventional search helps shoppers complete the transaction. The 23% is a journey conversion figure and should not be compared directly with session conversion rates. [Similarweb, 2025 AI visibility analysis](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/invest-in-ai-visibility/); [Similarweb, State of Ecommerce 2026](https://www.similarweb.com/corp/reports/state-of-ecommerce-2026/)

### AI influence exceeds what referral analytics can see

In a U.S. desktop-panel study, Similarweb followed users from a ChatGPT recommendation to behavior during the following seven days. Across finance, travel, and beauty, people recommended a brand were 2.5 times more likely to visit it than people recommended a competitor. Beauty is a consumer-commerce category, but this is not a broad retail-ecommerce sample and does not establish results for independent online shops. The study excluded users who had visited that site in the prior four weeks and users who named the brand in their prompt, helping isolate new recommendation exposure. Yet only 8.8% of those resulting visits came through an AI referral; most appeared in search (55.9%) or direct. The design shows downstream association under a controlled exposure comparison, not a randomized ad experiment. [Similarweb, Downstream Impact of AI Visibility](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/ai-visibility-downstream-impact/); [summary of channel mix and panel window](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/optimize-your-homepage-for-ai-traffic/)

Pew Research Center’s metered browsing study gives a useful U.S. consumer-adoption denominator without confusing AI mentions on pages with use of an AI answer engine. In March 2025, **13% of panel respondents** visited a generative-AI-tool site at least once during the month, rising to 20% among 18–29-year-olds. Around 58% encountered a Google search results page with an AI summary. The study analyzed browser activity for **900 U.S. adults**, recruited from KnowledgePanel with prior digital-metering participation; it covered desktop/laptop/mobile web browsing, not all in-app activity. Its 54% “shopping-site page with AI reference” measure is not shopping-AI use: it often counted retailer pages mentioning an AI feature. [Pew findings](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2025/05/23/what-web-browsing-data-tells-us-about-how-ai-appears-online/); [Pew methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/2025/05/23/methodology-metered-data-ai/)

Adobe’s November 2025 holiday companion survey found AI shoppers reported using AI links and feeling more confident with AI-assisted purchases, but the public holiday release does not expose the full question wording or sampling design for all those claims. Adobe’s later March 2026 survey of 5,000+ U.S. respondents found 80% of AI-shopping users said they use AI more than before and 79% said AI assistance increased purchase confidence. These are self-reports among AI users, not population-wide adoption rates or proof of completed purchases. [Adobe June 2026 report](https://business.adobe.com/uk/blog/adobe-report-ai-traffic-travel-sites-surges-200-percent)

### Merchant adoption is real; willingness to pay for GEO is unproven

The best directly relevant merchant evidence located is the **FHNW Online Retailer Survey 2025**, an online questionnaire fielded Apr. 21–Aug. 17, 2025. It had 643 shop operators, including 581 Swiss merchants (the target population), plus 27 Austrian, 20 German, and 15 other-country respondents. It was distributed through LinkedIn, newsletters, emails, and ecommerce contact forms, so it is a self-selected sample rather than a probability sample. 83% of respondents were B2C; the report says it is structurally representative of Swiss retailers across broad sectors. [FHNW study, November 2025](https://worldline.com/content/dam/worldline/local/en-ch/documents/251110_Zumstein_et-al_Online-Retailer-Survey_2025-final_en.pdf)

Within that sample, 83% already used AI to create or translate text, two-thirds used it for product descriptions, and merchants reported AI use for SEO/marketing. The distinction is important: adoption of generative copywriting and conventional search optimization is not the same as buying a GEO product. Only a minority analyzed AI-bot traffic or had GEO in their strategy. Of merchants who reported AI SEO use and answered its sales-impact question (n=299), 13% said no measurable sales increase, 17% reported 1–5%, 16% 6–10%, 9% 10–19%, 2% at least 20%, and 43% did not know or did not answer. That is weak but promising evidence of a problem: among self-selected AI-SEO users, measurement uncertainty itself is common. The survey measures reported AI SEO revenue impact, not verified uplift from a paid GEO tool. [FHNW, impact question](https://worldline.com/content/dam/worldline/local/en-ch/documents/251110_Zumstein_et-al_Online-Retailer-Survey_2025-final_en.pdf)

No public evidence reviewed gives a reliable share of merchants who would pay a named price for AI visibility or GEO software. U.S. Chamber of Commerce data says 58% of small businesses used generative AI in 2025, with broad perceived benefits, but it covers small businesses generally—not online merchants or GEO buying intent. It supports general openness to AI tooling, not product-market fit. [U.S. Chamber 2025 report](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/empowering-small-business-the-impact-of-technology-on-u-s-small-business)

### Platform competition can erase the simple product

Shopify’s December 2025 Agentic Storefronts announcement describes one setup that distributes merchant catalog data across AI chat surfaces, includes product, price, and inventory handling, and provides channel attribution and search-topic insights. Shopify says its catalog already uses signals from “millions of merchants and products.” This competes directly with the basic proposition “make my Shopify product feed available to AI” and reduces the value of standalone monitoring if Shopify exposes enough attribution. The opportunity has to go beyond feed syndication: diagnosing category-specific discovery gaps, fixing product/policy/schema/content issues across platforms, and tying work to attributable or experimentally measured outcomes. [Shopify Agentic Storefronts, 10 Dec 2025](https://www.shopify.com/news/winter-26-edition-agentic-storefronts)

## Reachable segment and profitability logic

Avoid a top-down TAM from AI shopping growth percentages. There is no defensible public count of stores that both need this product and can measure/pay for it. Start with a qualified beachhead:

- Independent ecommerce brands with a direct web shop, a differentiated catalog, and meaningful product-comparison or fit questions. High-consideration categories such as electronics, beauty, home, apparel and specialist goods have stronger evidence of AI use than low-involvement impulse categories. Adobe sees category differences; Similarweb finds specialist and comparison-focused brands can surface in AI answers.
- Merchants with enough sessions and orders to detect a change. A 0.4% current AI-referral baseline means low-traffic stores may see only a handful of AI referrals; statistical noise and subscription cost can swamp near-term benefit. Also include AI-influenced branded-search/direct conversions only where a test design can credibly measure them.
- Store owners, agencies, or ecommerce operators already paying for SEO/content/catalog tools and able to implement technical and editorial changes. Selling yet another dashboard to an owner who cannot change catalog data, product pages, or brand mentions is unlikely to create measurable ROI.

A merchant’s break-even math should use its own baseline, gross margin, and uplift—not general multipliers. For example, measure (i) AI-attributed sessions and orders, (ii) non-AI/search branded visits after observed AI visibility changes, (iii) gross profit rather than revenue, and (iv) hours saved. Require the expected monthly gross-profit contribution plus labor savings to clear the fee by a margin large enough to justify uncertainty. Public data does not justify plugging in a “54% uplift” as a causal forecast; that is a relative conversion-rate comparison among observed referrals, not a guarantee of extra orders from tool use.

Likely unit-economics shape: software delivery and automated monitoring can support healthy margins, but getting visibility changes may require recurring editorial, technical SEO, PR, catalog cleanup, integrations, and customer education. If results require hands-on work, a self-serve low price may not cover support; if support is removed, churn risk increases because rankings and recommendations are volatile and uplift is hard to attribute. A useful wedge is a measurable audit + prioritized fixes + monitoring with proof of implementation, priced for existing SEO budgets. Validate price, activation, renewal, and incremental margin before assuming scalable subscription economics.

## What would validate demand and profit

1. Interview merchants from a defined wedge and ask for recent, dated examples of AI referrals or recommendation losses, current tools/spend, and actions already funded. Do not use hypothetical “would you pay?” answers as validation.
2. Offer a paid audit/pilot and require payment or a signed budget commitment. Record conversion at a price, segment, and result; a waitlist is not willingness to pay.
3. Measure changes on a merchant cohort with matched pages/products or a staggered rollout: AI mention/citation, AI referral and branded-search visits, orders, gross profit, tool cost, and service time. Separate AI referrals from AI-assisted buyer activity later credited to search/direct.
4. Track renewal after 60–90 days and after first fixes. If customers will pay only for a one-off cleanup or agency service, price and package for that reality rather than presuming recurring software demand.
5. Test platform risk: Shopify’s native distribution/attribution and search platforms’ own merchant reporting may satisfy basic needs. Charge only for gaps they leave, such as competitor prompt share, missing attribute/content diagnostics, cross-platform change tracking, or experiments linking fixes to business outcomes.

## Product-specific implication for synthetic shopping-agent tests

A product that combines synthetic-agent testing, concrete catalog/page fixes, visibility tracking, and a fashion-fit workflow has a sharper wedge than a generic score. For fashion, tests could inspect whether an agent can distinguish exact variants, locate garment measurements and fit guidance, and accurately compare products against a shopper profile. This is a testable product hypothesis, not evidence that fashion merchants currently pay for it or that passing a synthetic test improves ranking. Synthetic agents can verify answerability and factual consistency against the store’s source data; they cannot stand in for real shoppers or predict the proprietary recommender ranking. Keep simulated answer quality, live visibility, referral/search behavior, and actual orders as separate outcome stages.

Reuters’ August 7, 2026 report specifically attributes to Adobe Analytics that 41% of U.S. consumers used generative AI for online shopping in June and that AI-referred visitors generated 41% higher revenue per visit than visitors from traditional channels. I located that Reuters report, but not a public Adobe release or methodology reproducing those June monthly figures. Adobe’s public June 17 update instead reports May 2026 retail referrals at +53% revenue per visit and +54% conversion; its June 29 release measures the June 23–26 Prime Day event and reports AI traffic converting 40% better, while saying AI traffic volume remained modest. These are different periods and metrics; leave Reuters’ reported June figures explicitly attributed to Reuters/Adobe Analytics and do not substitute the Prime Day event result for the full month. No basis was found for explaining the difference.
