# Does “start free and build a database” create an ecommerce AI SEO moat?

**Assessment date:** 28 September 2026

## Finding

A free audit can buy reach, feedback and product usage. It does not automatically buy a defensible data asset. A database of public product facts, prompt templates, synthetic shopper profiles, model answers and visibility snapshots is useful operationally, but competitors can collect or regenerate much of it. The potentially scarce dataset is longitudinal evidence linking a real intervention to a verified change in discovery or profitable sales. That evidence is also the data class with the hardest collection, permission, deletion and causal-validity problems.

For a Shopify app, there is a direct platform constraint: Shopify's API terms prohibit using Shopify-derived information, including anonymous, aggregated and derived data, to train or improve AI/ML without Shopify's prior written consent or the relevant merchant's consent for that merchant/store. The same terms separately prohibit competitive benchmarking with information from merchants or customers, and limit use of merchant data to the service for the merchant it relates to. Merchant consent for store-specific AI use should not be treated as blanket permission to pool data across stores or publish a benchmark. The pooled moat is therefore not an assumption to put in a pitch; it is a rights question to resolve with Shopify and merchants before collecting for that purpose. ([Shopify API License and Terms of Use, AI/ML restriction and Section 6.2](https://www.shopify.com/legal/api-terms))

## The distinction that matters: public or synthetic records versus intervention outcomes

A public/synthetic database can answer, “What did this crawler or test agent see?” It can support snapshots, test reproducibility, prompt coverage and merchant-specific recommendations. It does not by itself answer, “What change caused more profitable purchases?” The entries are reproducible, synthetic data can be regenerated, and generated explanations are hypotheses unless tested outside the same agent that produced the score.

Longitudinal intervention-outcome records are different. A useful record would include the exact change, the store and product version, when it went live, the tested prompt/model/locale and retrieval result, a comparison group or credible baseline, and later outcomes such as qualified visits, conversion, contribution margin and returns. Without a holdout or another credible comparison, a before/after increase can be caused by price, stock, promotion, seasonality, advertising, site changes or search/model changes. The moat would be in trustworthy measurement and valid access, not row count.

This distinction matters for the earlier proposal to make the audit free, store each store's information and ranking details, then learn what ranks better. Free distribution may increase the number of observations, but it does not turn public facts into exclusive data or synthetic rankings into causal evidence. More volume also does not fix the reuse restriction on Shopify-derived records.

## Shopify terms change the pooling assumption

Shopify defines “Merchant Data” broadly to include information relating to a merchant or store, including business, financial and product information and customer data. It says the app may use, access, store or copy this data only as necessary to provide its service to the merchant it relates to, as described to and limited by the merchant. It also says not to use information from merchants or customers for competitive benchmarking, and not to use information derived from the Shopify API, merchant data or a store, including anonymous, aggregate or derived data, to train, fine-tune or improve AI/ML without Shopify's written consent or consent from the relevant merchant for that merchant/store. ([Shopify API License and Terms of Use, definition of Merchant Data, AI/ML restriction and Section 6.2](https://www.shopify.com/legal/api-terms))

Practical consequence: a merchant-authorized app can hold the records needed to serve that merchant, and Shopify's text allows consent for AI improvement with respect to that merchant/store. That does not clearly authorize cross-merchant training or benchmarking. Because the separate competitive-benchmarking clause has no merchant-consent carveout in its text, do not promise pooled Shopify merchant benchmarks based only on an opt-in checkbox. Get Shopify's written position/authorization for that use. If the app's advantage depends on a multi-store benchmark, that dependency needs resolving before free-user data collection is presented as moat-building.

Shopify also requires public apps to handle `customers/data_request`, `customers/redact` and `shop/redact` webhooks. The app must complete required data requests within 30 days; Shopify sends `shop/redact` 48 hours after uninstall. Its API terms require deletion of Merchant Data copies within 30 days after uninstall, when data is no longer needed for the service, or on an enforceable delete request. Shopify's protected-data rules require minimum collection, stated purpose, retention periods and deletion support; more sensitive customer fields need approval and additional controls. ([Privacy law compliance](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/build/compliance/privacy-law-compliance), [protected customer data](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/protected-customer-data), [API terms](https://www.shopify.com/legal/api-terms))

These requirements make a raw, indefinite cross-store history a poor default design. Keep identifiable merchant records tenant-scoped, track provenance and retention, and make delete flows reach derived records, caches and backups where required. Preserve only properly anonymous, independently reusable outputs where the rights and anonymisation analysis support it; Shopify's contract restrictions can still apply to anonymous/derived data even when privacy law no longer applies.

## UK data-protection consequence

The ICO's principles require purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy and storage limitation. Pseudonymisation reduces risk but pseudonymous data remains personal data. Properly anonymised information falls outside data-protection law, but anonymisation itself is processing and requires a lawful basis and defined purpose; small cohorts or unusual products, places and dates can leave people identifiable when linked with other sources. ([ICO data protection principles](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/getting-started-with-gdpr/data-protection-principles-definitions-and-key-terms/), [ICO anonymisation guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/introduction-to-anonymisation/), [ICO pseudonymisation guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/pseudonymisation/))

For this product, the practical point is not to append customer/order analytics “for the moat” to a product-readiness app without changing the stated purpose, access design and retention model. Prefer product/store-level telemetry that does not identify shoppers when it can answer the product question. If shopper-level or order-linked records are genuinely required, keep them scoped to that merchant's service and delete them on schedule. A cohort benchmark needs both a lawful basis and a data-sharing right; removing names alone does not establish either.

## Free-user sample quality

A free plan produces a self-selected sample, not a random sample of ecommerce. It is likely to overrepresent merchants already worried about SEO/GEO, stores willing to install an app, owners with time to experiment and merchants who are not yet willing to pay. It may underrepresent mature teams with procurement/security review, merchants that already solved the issue, and shops too small or too busy to onboard. This is a sampling-risk inference, not a measured profile of this proposed product.

The useful response is to record the sampling frame and segment coverage: platform, country/language, product category, store/product scale, acquisition channel, activation and paid conversion. Keep pilot data labelled as free-user data. Do not treat its observed “what works” frequencies as general market truth unless the panel is broadened and results weighted or otherwise validated. Free installs also create survivor bias: stores that keep the app connected long enough to return data differ from those that uninstall quickly.

Free access may still be a sound acquisition experiment. Give away a bounded diagnostic, then test willingness to pay for scheduled monitoring, implemented fixes or a properly measured experiment. Do not justify free usage primarily as a way to harvest cross-merchant data until rights and representativeness are proven.

## Freshness and cost

Structured store facts are cheap to store; keeping them useful requires refresh and provenance. Record observation time, URL/product identifier, locale, crawl method and content hash. Keep a current snapshot plus necessary deltas rather than duplicating every unchanged page indefinitely. Search queries and generated answers need model/provider, model version where exposed, prompt, locale, date and tool configuration or comparisons will mix unlike runs.

The resource cost grows with breadth and repetition: stores × products/pages × prompts × engines/models × locales × runs. Repeated model/browser runs, screenshots, and order/session-level analytics will cost more to collect and operate than a compact table of product attributes. A free plan with unlimited monitoring can create recurring crawl/API expense before a customer proves willingness to pay. Meter prompts/products/refresh frequency or cap the free snapshot; quote no cost per run until the provider and measurement design are chosen.

The vendor also will not hold a durable history for you. OpenAI says API abuse-monitoring logs may include prompts and responses and are retained for up to 30 days by default; Responses API application state is retained for 30 days by default or when `store=true`, and some endpoints/features have different conditions. Store the records needed for your own longitudinal service in your own governed data store instead of relying on a model vendor as the archive. ([OpenAI API data controls](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data))

## What is actually defensible?

1. **A reliable measurement method.** Versioned tests, stable prompt panels, repeat runs where variance matters, clear distinction between simulated-agent outputs and real search/answer products, and external outcome checks. Competitors can copy a score formula; trust in calibration takes evidence.
2. **A merchant's own history and workflow.** Useful recommendations, approval/audit trail, before-and-after page versions, scheduled monitoring, and outcomes for that store can create switching costs and improve the service for that store. This is a product retention advantage, not a pooled data moat.
3. **A permissioned, representative outcome panel.** It could reveal which kinds of intervention work in which contexts. It requires explicit purpose and reuse terms, a credible platform right for Shopify-derived data, broad enough cohort coverage, ongoing participation and causal design. Until those are secured, describe it as a research hypothesis.
4. **Execution and distribution.** Free onboarding, low-friction integration, precise fixes and a credible proof of incremental contribution can be hard to reproduce in combination. The database supports this; it is not automatically the advantage on its own.

## Product implication

Use free service to learn whether merchants activate, apply a recommendation and pay for ongoing work. Make the initial data store useful even if cross-customer reuse is never permitted. Keep separate records for (a) public/synthetic test observations, (b) tenant-specific Shopify-derived service data, and (c) any separately permissioned analytics panel. Before treating (b) as a pooled training/benchmark dataset, obtain Shopify's written authorization and merchant permissions that explain the separate purpose. Build a real holdout or comparison design into experiments; label synthetic re-tests as simulations, not predicted sales lift.

A truthful pitch is: “We can build a better measurement product from repeatable tests and merchant-specific outcome histories. A cross-store benchmark could become valuable, but only if we earn valid, representative participation and platform permission.”
