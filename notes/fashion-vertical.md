# Fashion fit and measurement as an AI-commerce vertical

**Question.** Should an AI-commerce product start with fashion fit/measurement/catalogue semantics, or begin in a simpler attribute-heavy niche? This memo evaluates the earlier proposal in `shared-conversation-1.txt` and `shared-conversation-2.txt` against vendor documentation, published research, and current product/pricing claims. The commercial metrics below are identified as vendor assertions unless they come from the independent academic study.

## Recommendation

**Reject shopper-specific fit prediction and “we find the missing garment measurements” as the first product.** Fashion has a real, high-value information problem, but fit recommendation already has established data networks and product vendors. More critically, a catalogue QA tool can find that an inseam or hem measurement is missing; it cannot produce a truthful value from nothing. Collecting the value requires the brand, supplier, or operator to locate a tech pack or measure a physical sample. A product that reports gaps without closing them risks becoming a one-time audit and a human-services burden.

**Keep fashion only as a narrow, evidence-grounded catalogue QA pilot:** normalize and cross-check facts that already exist in the merchant’s source material (Shopify variants, supplier tech packs, size charts, product copy, and image sets); show the source and confidence for every extracted fact; flag missing or contradictory facts for human action. Do not predict “this will fit you,” generate unknown dimensions, or promise lower returns. If merchants will not pay for that workflow and supply source data, pivot the first vertical to a category with discrete, externally verifiable attributes, such as device/accessory compatibility (device model, generation, connector, protocol). This is a comparative hypothesis to test, not a proven market recommendation.

## The fashion niche has real pain, but the evidence is mixed

The strongest independent evidence located is a 2025 study of a Swedish high-end fashion platform: **496,365 items, 75,707 customers, 113 countries, July 2015–April 2022**. The platform’s size finder asked for self-reported body/build attributes and preferred fit. In the study, customers who used it were **0.65% more likely to return** an item; machine-learning feature analysis also ranked size-finder use among the least important return predictors. However, each unit quarterly increase in use on purchased items was associated with **7.51% higher customer lifetime value in the following quarter and 5.53% in the next**, suggesting engagement/retention value can coexist with more returns. These are observational results from one premium retailer and one system, not a causal estimate for all fit tools. [Study abstract and sample](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2444569X25001246), [full study](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/26e2f3b5-843b-4a78-ac53-ae03f18bd24e/content).

There is also a large-scale KDD paper describing production evaluation of a retailer’s SizeFlags fit-return monitoring system across 14 countries. It reports samples of **645,667 textile orders / 120,057 returns** and **503,849 shoe orders / 80,339 size-related returns** in its larger results section, with the overall model used to flag products whose size-related returns were anomalously high. This supports the value of return-feedback loops and human fit expertise, but is not evidence that static catalogue completeness by itself predicts fit. [SizeFlags paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03532).

The evidence says: fit matters, but prediction needs behavioural outcomes and domain feedback. “Sizing is a painful problem” does not establish that another app can solve it economically or that adding attributes alone improves returns.

**Claim discipline:** do not repeat any vendor sales/ROI percentages as independent findings. Use the exact metric, its source, who measured it, and the missing denominator or design caveat. In particular, conversion among users of a sizing tool does not establish that the tool caused conversion; people who open it are likely more purchase-ready. Prefer randomized holdouts, kept-item contribution after returns, and disclosed sample sizes.

## Can missing dimensions be obtained honestly?

Only when a trustworthy source exists. A system can extract dimensions from a size chart/tech pack, convert units, map variant rows, compare size progressions, check that a chart is linked to the correct style, or flag where an expected point-of-measure is absent. It can propose a likely interpretation only as an unverified hypothesis. It must preserve the distinction among **body measurement**, **finished-garment measurement**, and **recommended wearer range**; these are different data fields. ASTM’s apparel-sizing terminology treats body dimensions as a defined measurement domain, while True Fit’s technical material separately requires product catalog and sizing inputs plus shopper/history context. [ASTM D5219 scope](https://store.astm.org/d5219-09.html), [True Fit input requirements](https://www.truefit.com/fit-intelligence-spec).

If the merchant’s record and supplier data do not contain a garment waist, inseam, rise, hem, stretch percentage or tolerance, an AI cannot know that number. Someone must retrieve supplier/tech-pack information or measure a sample under a repeatable method. For each extraction, the product should store provenance (source file/URL, page or chart cell), units, size/variant scope, date, confidence and reviewer approval. Unknown stays **unknown**.

Product photos support some checks: image count, front/back/side/detail coverage, visible label text, color/shape cues, image resolution/crop and whether variants appear to have distinct photos. A normal uncalibrated image does not provide absolute physical scale. A paper on single-image height estimation explains the scale ambiguity caused by camera distance/focal length and reports an estimated human-height mean absolute error of 5.56 cm using learned body proportions, a large labeled dataset and explicit assumptions. This is not garment measurement validation; it underlines why a product image cannot honestly be presented as a tape measure. [Single-image height study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10355). Even if one physical dimension could be inferred from an in-frame reference, pose, drape, folds, perspective, lens and image processing would still require calibration and per-category validation.

## Separate data QA from fit prediction

| Product-data QA (defensible first scope) | Fit prediction (higher bar) |
|---|---|
| Is this product/variant discoverable? Is the size chart present and linked? Are size rows complete? Are “inches” and “cm” consistent? Are measurement labels body or garment? Are source values inconsistent across Shopify, supplier file and page? Does the image set show requested angles? | Given a particular shopper’s body, prior kept/returned items, desired ease, garment construction and size availability, which variant will fit and be kept? |
| Ground truth can be a supplied tech pack, chart, source page, Shopify feed, or approved merchant answer. | Ground truth needs fit intent plus actual purchase, kept/returned outcomes and reason codes, ideally across customers, products and brands. Customer self-report alone may be inaccurate or incomplete. |
| Honest output: cited finding + “missing / ambiguous / inconsistent” + human task. | Honest output requires a calibrated probability/recommendation and outcome validation, not just a confident LLM rationale. |
| Lower risk, but value depends on resolving issues and showing that agents/search actually use the data. | Established providers already have proprietary outcome graphs; false confidence risks bad fit, extra returns and customer trust. |

The proposed synthetic shopper score cannot substitute for outcomes. It can assess whether a defined agent can extract/compare the supplied facts on the tested pages and prompts. It cannot establish real-world fit or higher conversion unless tied to held-out external behavior. A generated “why competitor won” explanation is a hypothesis unless verified against data.

## Merchant cost and delivery burden

The subscription price is only part of cost. Data acquisition, catalog integration, variant mapping, measuring garments, tech-pack reconciliation, photo retakes, human approvals and keeping data in sync are likely the larger burden. Existing vendor examples show this in miniature: Kiwi reviews mention hands-on mapping of tech-pack calculations into recommendations; WAIR quotes merchant setup time and charges per URL plus recommender-attributed order; True Fit asks for a demo and connects catalog/sizing plus historical order/return data. No public Lily or True Fit self-serve price was found on the cited pages, so do not guess it. The fashion workflow becomes expensive when “we found the missing fields” actually means someone must measure a production sample for many style-size variants.

Also account for privacy and sensitive inference. Body measurements and fit profiles are personal data. The pilot should not ingest or infer shopper measurements; it should stay catalog-side until a separate legal/privacy review and data contract exist.

## Better first niche hypothesis

For a first agent-facing catalog QA test, compare fashion with **electronics/accessory compatibility** (for example, chargers/cases/adapters): exact supported device model/generation, connector type, protocol, power, dimensions and exclusions are discrete; many values can be checked against manufacturer documentation or model IDs; the evaluator can say “compatible / incompatible / unknown” without guessing a wearer’s subjective fit. There are still real risks—manufacturer data licensing, fast model turnover, hidden compatibility edge cases, product liability and existing compatibility databases—so this is a hypothesis to test with catalog owners, not a claim that the market is open or easy.

The selection criterion should be: *Can we verify missing/incorrect attributes from an authoritative source, with little merchant labor, and can the change be measured in an external shopping outcome?* If the answer is no, a niche may have lots of fields yet poor software economics.

## Pilot to make the choice

Run a 2–3 week paid concierge pilot before building a broad app. Recruit 3–5 Shopify brands in one fashion subcategory with enough repeat SKUs (e.g. denim or trousers) and 3–5 electronics/accessory sellers. Keep the same scope and operator time per merchant.

1. Import 100 randomly sampled product variants per merchant, stratified by category and available size/model. Ask the merchant for the authoritative source files (tech packs/charts or manufacturer compatibility sheets). Do not attempt to manufacture absent facts.
2. Independently double-label each field against those sources. Report extraction precision, variant-linking errors, contradiction detection, percentage of fields with no source, and minutes of human review per 100 variants. A false measurement/compatibility assertion should count as a serious error; do not average it away in a completeness score.
3. Test a fixed set of real shopper intents on the live page/feed and a clearly identified API/bot environment before and after approved fixes. Keep prompt set, inventory, price, time window and engine constant where possible. Report discovery, factual extraction and constraint satisfaction separately. Call it a benchmark, not a ranking or fit score for all AI systems.
4. Charge a real pilot fee or request a signed purchase commitment at a price that covers the human hours. Record which findings are actually fixed, time-to-fix, who did the work, and whether the merchant asks for a second run.
5. For fit claims, only proceed to a separate randomized controlled test once sample size, return reason capture and order-volume are sufficient. Track kept-item contribution and size-related returns, not just clicks or checkout conversion; disclose the denominator and analysis. The independent study shows these metrics can move in opposite directions.

**Pass fashion forward only if** most high-value findings can be resolved from existing source data (not newly measuring garments), extraction/variant mapping is reliably reviewable, merchants pay for the recurring workflow, and they can implement fixes within their current merchandising operation. **Choose the compatibility niche if** it materially lowers missing-source rate and review minutes while holding intent-to-purchase outcome measurement at least as strong. If neither passes, do not build the subscription around “AI readiness” scores.

## Bottom line

Fashion fit is a valuable domain but a poor first promise for a small entrant: fit prediction is an outcomes/data problem, not simply a vision/LLM problem, and established vendors already sell both size guidance and catalog enrichment. A tightly bounded, source-cited fashion data QA pilot remains testable. Start with checking and routing existing product facts, not creating measurements or predicting fit. Run it against one discrete-attribute niche before committing to the vertical.
