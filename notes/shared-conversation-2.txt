6. The hardest technical problem is whether your measurements mean anything

Your proposed product mixes several different outcomes:

Outcome	What measuring it would establish
A crawler can access the page	Technical accessibility
A product exists in a feed/catalogue	Distribution eligibility
A model can extract the correct attributes	Information quality
A product appears for sampled prompts	Visibility within that sample
A product is selected for a particular buyer	Recommendation suitability
A merchant receives an order	Attributed commerce
Your changes caused an otherwise absent profitable order	Incremental economic value

The dangerous product is one that measures the first four and sells the last one.

A. There is no single “ChatGPT rank”

OpenAI describes shopping results as influenced by the user’s request and context, including memory/custom instructions where applicable, alongside product information and factors such as price and availability. 
OpenAI Help Center

So “your visibility improved from 34 to 61” is incomplete without explaining the prompts, locations, context, dates, engine surfaces and weighting.

A merchant-specific prompt generator creates an additional problem: you can accidentally generate questions tailored to the catalogue you already know. That may produce reassuring scores without resembling actual buyer demand.

B. A homemade agent is not the production shopping system

An API model with web search, your own browser agent and the consumer shopping interface are different test environments. A result in one should not be represented as an observation in another.

There is a concrete example: Ahrefs explicitly says its Claude coverage uses the official Claude API with web search, and that increased costs restricted that coverage to custom prompts rather than its full dataset. 
Ahrefs

That is not an accusation against Ahrefs; it illustrates why methodology needs to be disclosed. Your software must say exactly what it tested.

Imagine an agent chooses a competitor, then tells you:

“Their product had clearer sizing information.”

That is a useful hypothesis. It does not establish that adding sizing information would change the production recommendation.

The actual difference could be availability, price, retrieval coverage, reputation or the sampled prompt. A persuasive generated explanation can be much easier to produce than a correct diagnosis.

D. My earlier counterfactual suggestion was overstated

I previously suggested improving an unpublished catalogue representation, re-running synthetic shoppers and showing predicted recommendation lift.

That is a simulation experiment—not a real-world A/B test.

It can tell you that your test system behaves differently when given different information. It cannot, by itself, establish that the real shopping platform will ingest the change, retrieve the product differently or generate additional purchases.

Without validation against live outcomes, the product risks becoming a system that writes content to impress its own evaluator.

E. More synthetic data is not automatically a moat

Twenty million synthetic shopping scenarios could still be cheap for a competitor to reproduce.

The harder assets would be representative real shopping intents, accurate product ground truth, linked purchase/return outcomes, and evidence that particular interventions improve those outcomes. Their value comes from validity and access—not merely volume.

F. Actual-channel monitoring has access and maintenance risks

Automated extraction from consumer interfaces cannot simply be assumed permissible or dependable. OpenAI’s consumer terms restrict automated/programmatic extraction, while business/API use is governed separately; the appropriate access arrangement needs checking for each channel. 
OpenAI
+1

This does not establish that competitors violate terms. It means “we will just automate every chatbot” is not a settled operating model.

7. Fashion makes the problem more valuable—but also much harder

This is where I most need to correct the previous enthusiasm.

True Fit already markets agents that understand detailed fit attributes and can supply fit intelligence to other agents. Its claimed advantage includes years of fit and purchase/return feedback. Lily already positions catalogue enrichment alongside testing. 
True Fit
+1

Your proposed fashion audit faces a fundamental issue:

Finding missing measurements is cheap. Obtaining reliable missing measurements is not.

A model cannot honestly fill in an unknown hem opening, garment waist or inseam because the merchant forgot to publish it.

There is also a distinction between body measurements and garment measurements. A waistband measurement is not automatically the intended wearer’s waist measurement; fit depends on ease, stretch, cut and preferences. A photograph without reliable scale and geometry does not uniquely determine physical dimensions.

Therefore, your app may produce a very accurate report whose action item is:

“Measure these hundreds of variants, resolve inconsistent supplier charts and photograph the missing views.”

That is potentially useful—but it transfers a sizeable operational task to the merchant.

The adversarial question becomes:

Will they pay you every month to repeatedly identify work they lack the resources to perform?

And when you move from “information completeness” to “this item will fit this person,” you take on a much more consequential accuracy problem. You would need evidence about actual fit and returns, not merely higher agent confidence.

8. Even genuinely useful recommendations might not justify the subscription

Here is an illustrative merchant scenario, not an estimate of current market averages.

Assumption	Value
Monthly store sessions	50,000
Assumed AI-referred share	0.5%
AI-referred sessions	250
Conversion rate on those visits	4%
Existing AI-referred orders	10
Additional orders caused by your app, assuming 20% lift	2
Contribution profit per incremental order	£25
Incremental monthly contribution	£50

A £99/month app would not pay for itself in this scenario—even though it genuinely improved the channel by 20%.

At £25 contribution per additional order, a £99 subscription needs roughly four genuinely incremental orders, before implementation effort. A £199 subscription needs roughly eight.

The relevant quantity is contribution after product cost, fulfilment, processing and expected returns—not gross sales.

Attribution makes this particularly easy to misunderstand

A dashboard saying “£2,000 revenue from ChatGPT” does not mean your app created £2,000.

Those orders might have arrived through Shopify’s native integration anyway. Or an unrelated promotion, price reduction, stock change or wider growth in AI shopping could explain the increase.

You need evidence separating:

“The channel produced sales” from “our intervention produced additional profitable sales.”

The channel itself should not be dismissed: Reuters reported Adobe data showing AI-referred visits generated 41% higher revenue per visit in June 2026. That supports the importance of AI shopping, not the effectiveness of any particular optimisation app.

10. Churn is the biggest missing financial variable
What I could not establish

I found no credible public product-level churn disclosure for the competitors examined.

For recently launched apps, there is also little elapsed time from which to establish long-term retention. Review counts and free-trial conversions cannot fill that gap.

What broader evidence suggests—and does not suggest

ChartMogul’s December 2025 analysis examined approximately 3,500 software businesses, including roughly 200 AI-native companies. Its annualized 2025 findings for AI-native products included:

Monthly price segment	Gross revenue retention	Net revenue retention
Under $50	23%	32%
$50–$249	45%	61%
Above $250	70%	85%

These are not Shopify-app benchmarks or competitor-specific churn rates. The dataset mixes business types, and revenue retention is not customer retention: a 23% gross revenue-retention figure does not mean exactly 77% of customers cancelled. 
ChartMogul

Still, it is a warning against assuming that low-priced AI experimentation naturally becomes durable recurring revenue.

What churn would do to your economics

Another hypothetical scenario: £49/month, 80% gross margin, constant monthly customer churn and no expansion.

Monthly customer churn	Original customers remaining after 12 months	Simplified gross-profit lifetime value
3%	69%	£1,307
7%	42%	£560
12%	22%	£327

The simplified lifetime-value calculation is monthly gross profit divided by churn; it excludes discounting, cohort variation and expansion.

At £49/month, approximately 205 paying customers gets you to £10,000 monthly recurring revenue. At 7% monthly churn, you need approximately 14 replacement customers every month just to remain flat.

That is before growth—and before paying yourself.

Your product has a plausible cancellation sequence:

Install → repair obvious issues → monitor for a while → see no clearly attributable improvement → cancel.

New catalogue items and changing engines could create recurring work. But you would need evidence that merchants value that recurring work enough to keep paying.

11. Other ways the business can fail even when the software works

The real bottleneck may be outside your control. Better product information cannot fix an uncompetitive price, unavailable stock or an unsuitable product. Google also explicitly says its AI search features do not require special AI text files or special schema beyond normal eligibility requirements. A proprietary checklist must not turn optional practices into invented necessities. 
OpenAI Help Center
+1

Free auditing may attract curiosity rather than buyers. A merchant checking a score is not necessarily willing to pay, implement changes or renew. Free scans also consume resources before there is demonstrated demand.

Automation can increase the downside. An erroneous measurement, material claim or shipping statement is more consequential than a slightly worse title. A responsible product needs evidence, approval and rollback—not simply a “fix everything” button.

Expanding platform support increases maintenance before proving demand. Supporting multiple catalogue structures, themes, languages and integrations does not resolve whether anyone values the result. It can simply multiply the implementation burden.

Moving upmarket is not a pricing toggle. Enterprise customers may support larger contracts, but you would then be selling against established providers and integrating with existing operational systems. The software, sales process and support expectations change together.

Agencies are a possible channel, but not a free acquisition strategy. Several competitors already offer agency or white-label functionality. An agency may want to reuse one licence across clients while expecting customised reporting and support.

These are business-model risks, not proof of inevitable failure. Collectively, however, they make “build a useful extension and charge monthly” an insufficient plan.

12. What would have to be true before I would stop rejecting it?

I would require four pieces of evidence—not another feature list.

1. Merchants pay after being shown the alternatives.
A pilot customer should know Shopify has native tools and that inexpensive competitors exist. Payment secured through ignorance of alternatives is weak validation.

2. You identify a consequential failure the alternatives miss.
Not a different score or more suggestions: a reproducible problem affecting real product selection, factual accuracy or profitable conversion.

3. Your intervention improves an external outcome.
Use live-channel observations and a credible experimental or comparison design. Do not use the same synthetic evaluator as both the optimiser and the sole judge of success.

4. Customers renew after the initial repair work.
Measure retained paying cohorts at 90 and 180 days, alongside actual testing and support costs. Renewal should reflect ongoing value, not merely an annual commitment that prevents immediate cancellation.

Until those conditions are demonstrated, I would not invest in a broad subscription product.

Bottom line

The original AI-readiness audit is commoditised. The more sophisticated version is already contested. The fashion version requires real product and fit data that a clever evaluator cannot manufacture.

The likely failure is not that you cannot build it. It is that you build something technically impressive which merchants enjoy trying, struggle to implement, cannot connect to incremental profit, and eventually cancel.

Your missing advantage is not another AI feature. It is credible evidence that you can produce a recurring, economically meaningful outcome better than the native tools and existing competitors.

That is the case I would try to disprove before writing the app.

Would a monthly watch for competitor launches, pricing changes and Shopify’s native features be useful?
