## Keep the model, correct its interpretation

The expected contribution per initial paid order is:

`M = P*(1-r) - (f*P+k) - C*(1+q) - L`

Here P is the complete customer payment, C is original delivered fulfilment, r is the fraction of initial orders fully refunded, q is a separate free-replacement frequency, and L is valued variable order labour. Full refunds remove the whole receipt. Original fees and fulfilment stay incurred; replacements add fulfilment. The base assumes no returned-stock recovery, supplier reimbursement, retailer-paid return postage or chargebacks. Partial returns need a different basket model. These assumptions describe losses; an 8% expected refund allowance does not fund the actual cash needed when two customers both require £72.

The denominator must remain initial paid acquired orders when comparing CAC with M. The economic memo's instruction to use retained orders only conflicts with its refund-adjusted initial-order model. Retained-customer CAC is also useful, but requires contribution expressed on that same retained-customer basis. For actual results, use the cohort's retained receipts and incurred costs directly, including refunded customers' acquisition spend. Never deduct ads once through CAC and again as fixed test spend.

## Cash admission is stricter than a unit-margin check

The previous contingency of two £72 refunds plus two £35.31 replacements is £214.62. Neither £100 nor £180 working cash covers it. £250 leaves £35.38, enough for only one £35.31 unfunded fulfilment with 7p left before extra costs. The £265 example leaves room for one such order and £15.07. Ten unfunded fulfilments plus that contingency require £567.72. These are stress examples, not safe order limits.

Before each accepted order, compare cleared cash with all unfunded fulfilments, refund exposure, committed remedies, due fees, taxes and likely return postage. A conservative early rule keeps refund cover at the greater of £144 or all still-exposed receipts, and replacement cover at the greater of £70.62 or committed remedies, then adds unfunded fulfilments and due costs. Increase cover as exposed orders accumulate. The £214.62 starting contingency must never be mistaken for a cap on legal liabilities. Do not count a pending payout as cleared cash or spend protected receipts on research. Reject a launch plan that relies on reducing reserve to fit an ad-platform minimum. ## What the diagnostic can earn

Organic orders are not a prerequisite. Paid exposure can be justified when the exact sample and repeat inspection pass, the offer is fulfilable, cheap usability checks have removed blocking faults, attribution works, and a specific buyer-access question remains. It needs an affordable accepted learning loss and enough working cash for any orders it creates. This research authorizes none of those external actions.

Use one platform, one crew, one delivered price, one page and up to two concepts over a chosen seven-day window, stopping sooner at £75 or any material fault. Confirm the platform can enforce that spend before launch. Calendar days diagnose delivery and purchase friction; they do not mature returns, establish recurrence or validate subscription demand. Do not automatically increase budgets 20% because day three looks good.

At the inherited expected-loss model, spending £75 requires at least three initial orders to cover variable costs and traffic, four to leave £5 per order, or ten to leave £18. For £100 the counts are four, five and thirteen; for £250 they are ten, twelve and thirty-two. These are arithmetic hurdles, not a statistical sample-size calculation. One actual refund in a small cohort can change the result sharply. CPC, purchase rate and reachable audience remain unknown.

For example, after £75 traffic, five initial crew orders imply £15 ad CAC and £11.03 unit contribution after ad cost. Yet with £148.22 sample/store/domain costs and twenty setup hours, the modeled round still loses £80.55 cash or £393.05 after all valued labour. Ten orders imply £7.50 ad CAC and £18.53 unit contribution, while the round shows £62.12 cash profit and a £262.88 economic loss. Those counts are illustrations; they are not plausible-volume claims.

Define portfolio economic profit as retained receipts minus actual fulfilment/remedies, original fees, all acquisition spend, shared costs, samples, variable labour and fixed founder labour. Include stopped products and failed ads. Define economic ROI explicitly as that profit divided by those consumed cash and labour resources, excluding held reserves. The five- and ten-order examples have approximately -54.3% and -28.4% economic ROI. A later product with a positive unit margin cannot erase earlier losses from the report.

A small result can justify one specific repair or a separately capped repeat. It can also leave demand unresolved because few relevant people saw the offer. It cannot warrant scaling without reconciled cohorts, actual costs, funded remedies and evidence that the next spend can recover its own costs. The fast-build method is useful when it saves page-production time for this sequence. Its reported store speed supplies no evidence for the quality, demand or cash gates.
